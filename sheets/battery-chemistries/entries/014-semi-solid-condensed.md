## Description
The pragmatic middle ground: cells that gel, densify, or drastically reduce the liquid electrolyte rather than eliminating it. Approaches include clay-like electrode slurries with electrolyte mixed in (24M's manufacturing-led version), heavily gelled "condensed" cells (CATL's ~500 Wh/kg aviation cell), and hybrid cells with in-situ-polymerized electrolytes. Marketed as "semi-solid" or "solid-liquid hybrid," they trade some of solid-state's promise for near-term manufacturability.

## Strengths and weaknesses
Far easier to produce than true solid state — most steps stay on conventional or simplified lines — while improving safety margin and enabling higher-energy electrode designs. Weaknesses: still flammable to a degree, so the safety story is incremental; "semi-solid" marketing obscures wildly different technical realities; cycle life at extreme energy density remains limited (aviation cells are hundreds of cycles).

## When to use
Pick semi-solid when you need higher energy or a wider safety margin now, on near-conventional manufacturing — aviation demonstrators and eVTOL programs chasing ~400–500 Wh/kg, range-flagship EV packs, or storage via 24M-style licensed lines — and can live with hundreds rather than thousands of cycles at the extreme densities. Avoid it if the requirement is true solid-state safety (these cells still burn) or commodity cost; interrogate each vendor's actual electrolyte content, and default to conventional Li-ion where its trajectory already meets the spec.

## Examples
CATL condensed-matter cell (civil aviation programs); WeLion semi-solid cells in NIO's 150 kWh swap pack; 24M licensees (Kyocera Enerezza storage, FREYR's former program).

## Economic profile
The value proposition is mostly cost-side: 24M-style processes remove drying and reduce inactive material; hybrid electrolytes reuse existing gigafactory capex. As a stepping stone it captures revenue now, but it competes directly with fast-improving conventional Li-ion rather than opening a protected niche.
