## Description
Solid-state cells using ceramic oxide electrolytes — garnets (LLZO), NASICON-types (LATP), perovskites — which are air-stable and electrochemically robust against lithium metal, at the cost of lower conductivity and brittle, hard-to-process ceramics. QuantumScape's flexible ceramic separator with an anode-free lithium-metal design is the best-known oxide approach; thin-film oxide cells have served niche microelectronics for years.

## Strengths and weaknesses
Wide electrochemical windows, stability in air (no H2S risk), and genuine compatibility with lithium-metal anodes. Weaknesses: ceramics must be sintered at high temperature and are brittle in thin, large-area formats; grain-boundary resistance and dendrite penetration along grain boundaries remain failure modes; scaling defect-free thin ceramic sheets to automotive areas is a hard manufacturing problem.

## When to use
Choose the oxide route when air-stable processing and genuine lithium-metal compatibility outweigh conductivity — thin-film microbatteries, medical implants, premium consumer cells, or licensing plays like QuantumScape-PowerCo where someone else carries the ceramics capex. Avoid it if you need near-term automotive volume on your own line: sintering yield on large thin ceramic sheets is unsolved at that scale, and the sulfide route or semi-solid cells are the likelier path there.

## Examples
QuantumScape QSE-5 samples (VW/PowerCo licensing), ProLogium's oxide-based cells (FID with Mercedes), thin-film Cymbet/STMicro microbatteries, Ilika medical cells.

## Economic profile
The manufacturing challenge is ceramics-industry economics grafted onto battery throughput: sintering furnaces, yield on thin fragile sheets, and areal throughput per line. Licensing models (QuantumScape-PowerCo) spread the capex risk. Wins first where safety and density justify premium pricing — consumer, medical implants, then vehicles.
