## Description
A conversion chemistry: a lithium-metal anode against a sulfur-carbon cathode, where sulfur converts through a cascade of lithium polysulfides to Li2S. Sulfur is nearly free, cobalt- and nickel-free, and offers theoretical energy density several times lithium-ion's; practical cells reach 350–450 Wh/kg at very low weight. The defining problem is the polysulfide shuttle — soluble intermediates migrating to the anode, self-discharging the cell and destroying both electrodes.

## Strengths and weaknesses
Exceptional gravimetric energy and dirt-cheap, abundant cathode material with a clean supply chain. Weaknesses: poor volumetric energy density (sulfur cathodes are fluffy); cycle life historically ~100–300; the shuttle effect and lithium-metal anode combine two hard problems in one cell; rate capability is modest. Recent carbon-scaffold and sparse-electrolyte designs are pushing life toward usable ranges.

## Examples
Lyten (Stellantis investment, defense and drone cells), Theion, Zeta Energy (Stellantis development deal); historic Sion Power/Airbus Zephyr HAPS flights ran on Li-S.

## Economic profile
Bill-of-materials cost could undercut LFP while doubling its energy density — the most disruptive cost-per-kg story in batteries if cycle life closes. Near-term revenue is drones, defense, and aviation, where weight prices dominate. Volumetric density keeps it out of phones and makes EV packaging harder than the Wh/kg headline suggests.
