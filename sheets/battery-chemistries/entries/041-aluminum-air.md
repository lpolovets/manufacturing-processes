## Description
A primary metal-air system with spectacular theoretical numbers: aluminum anodes oxidize to hydroxide at an air cathode, offering practical energy densities of 400–800 Wh/kg. Aluminum cannot be electrically recharged in aqueous cells — the battery is "refueled" by mechanically swapping anodes and electrolyte, with the spent hydroxide recyclable back to metal through the existing aluminum smelting industry.

## Strengths and weaknesses
Very high energy density from the cheapest structural metal; instant "recharge" by cassette swap; storable indefinitely dry. Weaknesses: parasitic corrosion consumes the anode even at rest once wetted (mitigated by alloying and electrolyte additives); mechanical recharging demands a swap-and-recycle logistics network that has never materialized; round-trip energy economics via re-smelting are poor; power density is modest.

## When to use
Pick aluminum-air for reserve power that must store dry for years and then deliver high energy on activation — military reserve packs, unattended sensors, emergency range extension — where mechanical anode swap is operationally acceptable or the battery is single-mission. Avoid it for anything requiring electrical recharge or lacking a swap-and-recycle logistics chain (the requirement that has sunk it commercially for forty years); Li-SOCl2 covers long-dormancy unattended power, and Li-ion covers everything rechargeable.

## Examples
Military reserve and unattended-sensor power (Epsilor-style products); Phinergy's automotive range-extender demonstrations with Renault-Nissan and IOC India; telecom backup pilots in India.

## Economic profile
Recurrent commercial near-misses for forty years: the electrochemistry works, but the business model requires infrastructure (anode swap, hydroxide return) that no single customer can justify. Viable today only in defense reserve-power niches; any "aluminum economy" pitch should be tested against that logistics burden.
