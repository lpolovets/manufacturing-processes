## Description
A molten-electrode battery operating at 300–350 °C: liquid sodium anode, liquid sulfur/polysulfide cathode, separated by a solid beta-alumina ceramic electrolyte that conducts sodium ions. Both electrode materials are among the cheapest on earth, and the all-liquid electrodes suffer none of the structural fatigue that limits solid electrodes. NGK Insulators has operated fleet-scale Na-S storage since the early 2000s — the original long-duration grid battery.

## Strengths and weaknesses
Cheap, abundant active materials; 4,500+ deep cycles; ~6-hour discharge durations; two decades of fielded gigawatt-hours. Weaknesses: must be kept at ~300 °C (standby heating losses); beta-alumina tubes are the cost and reliability bottleneck; a cracked electrolyte puts molten sodium and sulfur in contact — a 2011 fire at a Japanese plant forced fleet-wide redesigns; cold starts are slow and stressful.

## When to use
Consider Na-S only for utility-scale, 4–8 hour storage cycled daily — the continuous duty that keeps the 300 °C stack warm on its own losses — and realistically only within NGK's established supplier relationship, where two decades of fielded fleet data de-risk the ceramic-tube failure mode. Avoid it for intermittent or standby duty (heating losses kill the economics) and for new greenfield procurement, where LFP containers now underbid it; the chemistry is bought on track record, not on a cost roadmap.

## Examples
NGK Insulators systems: hundreds of MW deployed for Japanese utilities, Abu Dhabi's 108 MW/648 MWh virtual plant — long the world's largest battery installation; BASF Stationary Energy Storage partnership.

## Economic profile
Proven levelized economics for 4–8 hour daily cycling, but the technology never achieved a cost curve — one dominant supplier, ceramic-tube manufacturing that resisted scaling, and now direct assault from ever-cheaper LFP containers. Instructive as the cautionary tale that early long-duration leadership doesn't survive a rival's learning curve.
