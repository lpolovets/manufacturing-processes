## Description
Flow batteries that replace mined metals with synthesized molecules: quinones, viologens, ferrocene derivatives, and other redox-active organics dissolved in water or organic solvent. The dream is a designer electrolyte manufactured from commodity chemical feedstocks at whatever scale the grid demands, with properties (voltage, solubility, stability) tuned molecule by molecule. Adjacent efforts include hydrogen-bromine and polysulfide-air chemistries.

## Strengths and weaknesses
Potentially very cheap active material decoupled from any mining supply chain; tunable chemistry; aqueous versions are non-flammable. Weaknesses: molecular decomposition — organic electrolytes degrade over months-to-years in ways vanadium never does, and "calendar fade" of the electrolyte is the field's defining unsolved problem; energy density is low; no organic system has meaningful commercial field history.

## When to use
Treat organic flow as a pilot-and-research choice, not a procurement option: engage it through funded demonstrations or materials investments betting that electrolyte-from-chemical-plants beats mined vanadium, and in any deal model electrolyte replacement as an operating cost until a system shows decade-scale molecular stability in the field. For storage that must be deployed and financed now, use VRFB among flow chemistries or LFP outright; nothing organic has meaningful commercial field history yet.

## Examples
Quino Energy (aqueous quinones, Harvard lineage), Kemiwatt and JenaBatteries/CERQ in Europe, XL Batteries' organic system; Lockheed Martin's GridStar Flow (coordination chemistry) sits nearby.

## Economic profile
A materials-science venture bet layered on an already-hard systems business. The pitch — electrolyte from chemical plants, not mines — is compelling if stability crosses the decade threshold; until then, every deal should price electrolyte replacement as an operating cost, not a one-time capex.
