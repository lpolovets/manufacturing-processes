## Description

A monocrystalline diamond tool machines optical-quality surfaces on a precision lathe.

## Strengths and weaknesses

Produces nanometer-scale finishes and complex aspheric or freeform geometry. Ferrous materials degrade diamond tools, and vibration and temperature control are demanding.

## When to use

Diamond turn when the optic is metal, crystal, or polymer and the geometry is aspheric, freeform, or off-axis: aluminum and copper mirrors, germanium and zinc selenide infrared lenses, and the mold inserts behind molded polymer optics all come off the machine with single-digit-nanometer roughness and submicron figure, no polishing lab required. It is the fastest path from CAD to a finished asphere at prototype and low-to-medium volume. Chemical tool wear rules out steel and most ferrous alloys — plate them with electroless nickel and turn the plating instead — and glass and other brittle materials generally still need grinding and polishing. For visible-wavelength systems where residual turning marks scatter light, plan a post-polish or use MRF; for high-volume polymer optics, use SPDT for the mold and let injection molding make the parts.

## Examples

Infrared optics, mirror substrates, laser components, optical molds.
