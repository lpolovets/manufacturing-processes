## Description
The research hand re-engineered for shifts, not papers: 6–16 actuated DoF (deliberately fewer than research maximalism), tendon or linkage drives packaged into forearms, fingertip tactile arrays, and — the actual innovation — design for manufacture, service, and replacement at fleet scale. Tesla Optimus, Figure, and their peers treat the hand as the robot's critical path: their bet is that a single sufficiently-good hand plus learned policies beats the task-specific-tooling paradigm that built industrial automation.

## Strengths and weaknesses
Covers a broad task distribution with one SKU (the entire economic thesis of general-purpose robots); underactuation and compliant surfaces buy robustness research hands lacked; integrated wrists and quick-swap service models acknowledge reality. Weaknesses: still the least reliable subsystem of every humanoid — fingers meet the world first and hardest; tactile durability and tendon life remain the wear story; per-hand costs (thousands) times two times fleet size is real money; and the "sufficiently-good" threshold keeps moving as tasks harden.

## Examples
Tesla Optimus Gen-2/Gen-3 hands (11-DoF tendon design, publicly agonized-over), Figure's hands through their 02/03 iterations, Sanctuary Phoenix's hydraulic fingers, 1X's compliant hands, Sharpa and Chinese humanoid hand suppliers emerging as a component tier.

## Economic profile
The component battle of the humanoid era: if general-purpose robots ship at volume, hands are a tens-of-millions-of-units market with consumable fingertips — and a supplier tier is already forming ahead of demand. The counter-scenario is equally live: humanoids that mostly do warehouse picks may quietly ship with two-finger grippers and swap hands per task, collapsing the premium. Watch what fraction of deployed humanoid tasks actually use more than a pinch.
