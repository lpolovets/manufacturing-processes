## Description
The workhorse of world logistics: elastomer cups pressed against a surface while vacuum — from venturi ejectors or pumps — clamps the object with atmospheric pressure itself. One accessible face is the entire requirement. Cups attach in milliseconds, tolerate centimeters of positioning error, weigh grams, and cost dollars; arrays of them lift everything from single envelopes to full appliance panels. Modern picking systems pair cup arrays with vision and flow sensing for per-cup grip verification.

## Strengths and weaknesses
Fastest attach in gripping, single-face access, huge pose tolerance, near-zero moving mass, cheap failures (cups are consumables), scales from grams to hundreds of kilos via arrays. Weaknesses: porous, curved, dusty, oily, or crumpled surfaces leak; the compressed-air bill is real (venturis are convenient and thermodynamically offensive); grip is normal-force only — shear and peel are the failure directions; hiss and dust movement disqualify some cleanrooms; and every dropped parcel in e-commerce is a leak that won.

## Examples
Piab, Schmalz, and SMC ecosystems (cups, ejectors, sensors); Amazon Robin/Cardinal/Sparrow's suction arrays; every palletizer and carton erector; sheet-metal and glass handling lines; egg and produce packing with food-grade silicone cups.

## Economic profile
A consumables-rich oligopoly (Piab, Schmalz) with razor-blade economics — cups wear out by design — plus an energy-efficiency upgrade cycle (smart ejectors with air-saving control) selling opex reduction. As the incumbent technology of robotic picking, it is the benchmark: any novel gripper pitch must state precisely which objects vacuum fails on and how many of those exist per thousand picks.
