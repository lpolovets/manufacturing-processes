## Description
Grip by surface physics: electroadhesive pads induce electrostatic attraction against almost any material — fabric, foil, cardboard, glass — with kilovolts at microamps, while gecko-inspired dry adhesives use micro-structured fibrils whose van der Waals contact switches on with shear load and releases with a peel. Both grab where vacuum leaks and magnets shrug: porous textiles, perforated sheets, in-vacuum wafers, and space debris.

## Strengths and weaknesses
Works on porous and non-ferrous surfaces, nearly zero energy (electrostatic) and truly zero energy (gecko), silent, no air, functions in hard vacuum — hence the space and semiconductor interest. Weaknesses: holding pressures are modest (fractions of vacuum's), so payloads per area disappoint; electroadhesion charges dust to the pad and degrades with humidity; gecko fibrils foul and wear, needing cleaning cycles; release timing (charge decay) complicates fast cycles; and industrial track records remain thin.

## Examples
Grabit's electroadhesive sheet handlers (Nike shoe-upper stacking — the famous win, and a company that faded), gecko grippers on Stanford/JPL space-debris demos and ISS experiments, OnRobot's briefly-sold Gecko line, textile and composite-ply handling pilots.

## Economic profile
Perennially promising, commercially fitful: each mechanism owns real niches (limp textiles, in-vacuum handling) that are individually small, and the flagship vendors have repeatedly stalled. The apparel-automation prize (sewing's limp-fabric problem) keeps venture interest alive. Right posture: application-specific bets where vacuum physically cannot compete, not general-gripping stories.
