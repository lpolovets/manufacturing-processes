## Description
The same TRISO fuel and helium coolant as the pebble bed, but with particles pressed into compacts and stacked in hexagonal graphite blocks — a fixed, machined core rather than a flowing one. Prismatic designs trade the pebble bed's on-line refueling for precise core geometry, higher achievable outlet temperatures (Japan's HTTR has run 950 °C — hot enough for thermochemical hydrogen), and none of the pebble-flow and dust issues.

## Strengths and weaknesses
Highest demonstrated outlet temperatures in fission, well-characterized core mechanics, and the same walk-away TRISO safety case. Weaknesses: batch refueling with complex block shuffling; graphite block machining and replacement costs; the same low power density and big-vessel economics as all HTGRs; and a thinner commercial bench than the pebble-bed camp — the design's fifty-year history (Peach Bottom, Fort St. Vrain's troubled run, GA's cancelled GT-MHR) is rich in prototypes and poor in fleets.

## Examples
Japan's HTTR (operating, 950 °C demonstrations, coupled hydrogen-production testing); Fort St. Vrain (US, 1979–89, plagued by water ingress); Ultra Safe Nuclear's MMR (Canadian licensing at Chalk River before the company's 2024 bankruptcy and asset sale); General Atomics' prismatic lineage.

## Economic profile
Sits in the pebble bed's shadow commercially: the same heat markets, less momentum. Its distinctive asset is the 950 °C capability — the only demonstrated fission route to sulfur-iodine hydrogen and the hottest industrial heat. USNC's bankruptcy is the sector's caution: TRISO-based microreactor ventures burn capital on fuel qualification long before revenue.
