## Description
The Gen-IV roadmap's hardest entry: helium coolant (transparent, inert, no activation) combined with a fast spectrum and 850 °C outlet — on paper uniting HTGR temperatures with breeder fuel economy. The physics problem is decay heat: a fast core has high power density and helium has almost no thermal inertia, so losing pressure means losing cooling within seconds. Solving depressurized decay-heat removal without water or sodium's natural circulation has defeated every design iteration since the 1960s.

## Strengths and weaknesses
Chemically inert coolant with direct-cycle turbine potential, full fast-spectrum fuel-cycle benefits, no sodium fires or lead corrosion. Weaknesses: the decay-heat problem requires either massive passive injection systems or fuel that survives 1,600 °C+ uncooled — carbide fuels that barely exist; no GFR has ever been built, anywhere; and every practical compromise pushes the design back toward the thermal HTGR it was meant to surpass.

## Examples
Purely programmatic: Euratom's ALLEGRO demonstrator concept (Czech/Slovak/Hungarian consortium, perpetually pre-decision), historic GA and UK GCFR studies, Gen-IV Forum working groups.

## Economic profile
No credible commercial vehicle exists; the GFR persists as a research placeholder for "what if HTGR safety met breeder economics." Its analytical value is as a boundary case: the design shows why coolant thermal inertia — not elegance — decides which advanced reactors are buildable. Watch only if refractory carbide fuels mature elsewhere (space reactors may inadvertently qualify them).
