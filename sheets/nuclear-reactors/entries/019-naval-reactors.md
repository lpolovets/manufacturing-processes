## Description
The most successful nuclear program in history by operational record: compact PWRs driving submarines and carriers, with the US fleet logging 5,700+ reactor-years and zero radiological accidents. US cores use weapons-grade HEU in robust cermet fuel, achieving 30+ year lifetimes with no refueling — a submarine now outlives its reactor fuel question entirely. France runs LEU boats (refueling every ~10 years); AUKUS is exporting the US/UK HEU approach to Australia.

## Strengths and weaknesses
Extreme power density, shock tolerance, decades-long cores, silent natural-circulation operation at low power, and an institutional safety culture (Rickover's) that civilian nuclear still studies. Weaknesses: everything is classified, gold-plated, and cost-insensitive — almost nothing transfers directly to civilian economics; HEU fuel is a proliferation headache that pressures future designs toward LEU at real performance cost; and the industrial base (two US yards, one UK, one French) is a chronic bottleneck.

## When to use
The procurement scenario is singular: you are a navy (or an icebreaker fleet) for whom power density, endurance, and stealth justify defense-budget pricing — no civilian buyer faces this menu. The civilian use is as evidence and lineage: proof that small PWRs can be serial-built to immaculate quality, the workforce behind Rolls-Royce's SMR credibility, and Russia's RITM-derived floating and land units, the one direct naval-to-civilian product line. Draw cost lessons with care — nothing in these programs was ever optimized for $/MWh.

## Examples
US Virginia- and Columbia-class (life-of-ship cores), UK Astute/Dreadnought (Rolls-Royce PWR3), France's Suffren class (LEU K15 lineage), China's expanding fleet, Russia's icebreakers (the civilian cousin — RITM-200 reactors now spun into floating and land SMR offerings).

## Economic profile
Militarily priceless, commercially instructive: naval reactors prove small reactors can be serial-built with immaculate quality — at defense budgets. The civilian spillover is real but indirect: Rolls-Royce's SMR credibility, Russia's RITM-based SMRs, and the trained workforce. AUKUS-driven HEU expansion versus LEU conversion research (a US congressional perennial) is the fuel-policy tension to watch.
