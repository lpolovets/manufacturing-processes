How to evaluate a reactor design

Reactor physics is rarely the hard part. Nearly every design in this sheet works, and most of them worked in a national lab decades ago. What separates deployed fleets from perpetual concepts is everything around the core: capital cost and construction risk, fuel-cycle logistics, regulatory pathway, and the arithmetic of decade-long timelines running against competitors that improve every year.

Engineering factors

FactorWhy it matters
Coolant & moderatorThe choice of water, gas, sodium, lead, or salt cascades into everything: operating temperature, pressure, materials, safety case, and what can go wrong.
Outlet temperature~300 °C (LWR) makes electricity at ~33% efficiency; 550–950 °C (sodium, salt, gas) unlocks better cycles plus industrial heat, hydrogen, and synfuel markets.
Pressure155-bar PWR vessels drive containment size and forging supply chains; low-pressure salt and sodium systems shrink both — one of the genuine advanced-reactor advantages.
Passive safetyDesigns that remove decay heat with no power, operators, or moving parts (SMRs, HTGRs, salt) simplify siting, emergency planning zones, and public argument.
Neutron spectrumThermal reactors need moderation and burn ~1% of mined uranium; fast reactors breed and burn actinides but need high-fissile startup cores and harder materials.
Materials & corrosionSodium burns in air, salts corrode alloys, helium leaks, lead erodes pumps; forty years of "advanced" delays live in these details.
Load followingGrids with heavy renewables want flexible output; xenon dynamics and thermal stress limit some designs more than others.
Fuel form & burnupTRISO particles, metal fuel, and liquid salts each trade fabrication cost against burnup, safety margin, and waste form.

Economic and strategic factors

FactorWhy it matters
Overnight capex ($/kW)The headline number — but Western FOAK projects (Vogtle ~$15k/kW, Flamanville, Hinkley) ran 2–3× estimates while China and Korea build the same physics at $2.5–4k. The gap is execution, not design.
Construction time & interestAt nuclear capital intensity, financing cost compounds brutally: a 12-year build can double effective cost. Schedule certainty is worth more than thermal efficiency.
FOAK → NOAK curveEvery vendor quotes the nth-of-a-kind price; nobody sells you the nth unit first. Serial factory production (SMRs) is a bet that manufacturing learning can replace site-built learning that never materialized in the West.
Fuel-cycle logisticsLEU is a functioning global market. HALEU (5–20%), which most advanced designs need, has almost no Western supply — Russia dominated it — making enrichment buildout a gating dependency outside the vendors' control.
Regulatory pathwayUS NRC licensing runs years and hundreds of millions of dollars; novel coolants must effectively teach their regulator the technology. Jurisdictional arbitrage (Canada's CNSC, national programs) is a real strategy.
Waste & decommissioningPolitically unsolved in most of the West; designs that burn actinides or produce better waste forms claim an advantage that markets don't yet price.
Demand anchorThe 2023–25 wave of hyperscaler power deals (data centers) changed nuclear's revenue question from "can it beat gas?" to "who pays a premium for firm clean power?" — the most important commercial shift in decades.
Proliferation & export controlEnrichment level, plutonium handling, and reprocessing decide who may buy, host, or finance a design.

Nuclear timelines carry a systematic optimism bias measured in decades. A good rule of thumb is that first-of-a-kind dates slip 5–10 years from announcement, and "commercial fleets" follow first units by another decade.

Fission versus fusion, honestly

Fission is a deployment problem: the technology works, the fleet exists, and the question is whether anyone outside Asia can build it on budget. Fusion is still a physics-and-engineering problem: net energy gain was demonstrated (NIF, 2022) but no approach has yet produced net electricity, sustained it, or survived its own neutron flux for years. Private fusion's ~$8B+ funding wave is a bet that superconducting magnets (REBCO tape), modern computation, and startup cadence compress a 40-year government roadmap into 15. The serious diligence questions differ between the two. For fission, ask about construction execution and HALEU. For fusion, ask about tritium breeding, first-wall materials, duty cycle, and what the levelized cost is even supposed to be.

Core takeaway

Reactor designs usually fail commercially for reasons that appear nowhere in the reactor-physics textbook: construction overruns, fuel that can't be bought, regulators that have to be taught, and markets that won't wait. Evaluate any design as four bets at once (thermal-hydraulics, supply chain, licensing pathway, and revenue model), and weight the last three at least as heavily as the first. Coolant choice is a poor predictor of cheap nuclear. The good predictor is whether someone gets to build the same thing many times.

Key questions for engineering decisions

  • Does the coolant-materials combination have demonstrated years of operation, or only computed ones?
  • Is the safety case passive, and does it hold at the actual siting (emergency planning zone, water, seismic)?
  • Can the design load-follow and integrate with the grid or heat customer it claims?
  • What is the fuel qualification status (enrichment, fabrication, and burnup data), as opposed to just the fuel concept?

Key questions for investment and business analysis

  • What did the last comparable project actually cost per kW, and why will this one differ?
  • Where does the fuel come from (HALEU or tritium specifically), and who has actually committed the supply?
  • Which regulator, which pathway, and what has the vendor already filed (versus announced)?
  • Who is the anchor customer, and does the offtake price support FOAK economics or only NOAK projections?
  • What is the credible slip on the stated timeline, and does the balance sheet survive it?
  • Is the moat design IP, licensing progress, fuel access, or manufacturing? And which of those actually compounds?

Durable advantage in nuclear has historically gone to whoever builds repeatedly under one regulator with one supply chain (France in the 1980s, Korea in the 2000s, China now). A new design competes with gas and renewables, and it also has to compete with that track record.
