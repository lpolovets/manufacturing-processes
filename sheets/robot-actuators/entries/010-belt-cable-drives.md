## Description
Transmission at a distance: synchronous belts, steel cables, and capstan windings carry torque from a motor mounted where mass is cheap (the base) to a joint where mass is expensive (the wrist). Cable and capstan drives add near-zero backlash and superb backdrivability — friction is rolling, not sliding — which made them the choice of haptics and surgical robotics, where feeling the load is the entire product.

## Strengths and weaknesses
Remote actuation slashes limb inertia; smooth, quiet, backdrivable, and backlash-free (tensioned cables); belts are cheap and forgiving of misalignment. Weaknesses: compliance — belts stretch and cables creep, stealing stiffness and demanding tensioning maintenance; routing through multiple joints couples them kinematically; power density is modest; and cable fatigue/fray is a wear item with inspection burden (surgical robots replace instruments partly for this reason).

## Examples
The da Vinci surgical system's cable-driven instruments (the category's flagship), Phantom haptic devices' capstans, 3D printer CoreXY belt systems, SCARA and delta robot belt stages, tendon-driven robot hands (Shadow Hand), Agility Robotics' leg transmissions mixing cables and links.

## Economic profile
Belts are a commodity; precision cable/capstan work is artisanal engineering with real know-how moats (tension management, termination, routing) but little standalone industry — it lives inside products like surgical and haptic systems. For humanoid hands, tendon drives are the incumbent compromise between dexterity and packaging, keeping this old craft strategically current.
