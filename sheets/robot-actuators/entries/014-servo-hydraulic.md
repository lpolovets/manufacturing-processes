## Description
Pressurized oil at 200–350 bar metered by servo valves into cylinders and rotary actuators: the highest force density any actuation technology delivers, by an order of magnitude. A fist-sized cylinder exerts tonnes; power routes through hoses to joints that weigh almost nothing themselves. Hydraulics built every excavator, press, and aircraft control surface — and powered the era-defining dynamism of Boston Dynamics' hydraulic Atlas and BigDog before the company itself went electric.

## Strengths and weaknesses
Unmatched force and power density at the joint, intrinsic overload tolerance (relief valves), stiff force transmission, robust in filth and heat. Weaknesses: the system tax — pump, accumulator, valves, hoses, filters, and heat exchanger — erases the density win at small scale; servo valves cost thousands and demand immaculate fluid; efficiency is dismal (throttling losses; often 10–30% system-level); leaks are endemic; and force control through valve nonlinearity is hard-won.

## Examples
Hydraulic Atlas's backflips (retired 2024 — the symbolic end of hydraulic humanoids), every excavator and forestry machine (now targets for robotic retrofit — Gravis, Built Robotics), aircraft actuation, industrial testing rigs (MTS/Instron), die-casting and forging automation.

## Economic profile
Hydraulics rule where force is enormous and mass budget generous — construction, mining, presses — a vast mature industry now being retrofitted with autonomy rather than replaced. In legged robotics the verdict is in: electric QDD matched the dynamism at a tenth the maintenance. The surviving frontier is electro-hydrostatic hybrids (next entry) and autonomy retrofits of existing hydraulic fleets, where the machine is already bought.
